ART STYLE: ILLUSTRATED STORYBOOK, HAND-DRAWN COLORED PENCIL

1. ESSENCE, AND WHAT IT IS NOT
A hand-illustrated winter storybook that has come to life, drawn in
colored pencil on warm toothy paper by a skilled illustrator. The
screen is the world itself, full frame. Quiet, cozy, nostalgic,
refined. Every frame reads at a glance through strong silhouettes and
soft shapes.
It is NOT child art (no scribbled fills, no wobbly child perspective),
NOT glossy clean app vector art, NOT a watercolor painting (the wash is
a soft guest under the pencil, the pencil is the host), and NOT shown
inside a book (no pages, edges, binding, or page turns). No felt,
fabric, or paper-cutout textures.

2. MATERIALS AND RENDERING
Build these as rendering rules, not as a filter over flat vectors.
- Paper: warm off-white, three octaves of value noise plus a faint
  horizontal fiber term, low mottling (about 2%), soft warm vignette.
  The tooth is anchored to the world, so it pans with the camera and is
  never fixed to the screen. A subtle emboss lit from the top left,
  weaker where pencil has filled the tooth.
- Pencil adhesion (the core rule): every pencil layer stores color plus
  pressure. Pigment lands only on the peaks of the paper tooth. Light
  pressure gives a fine speckled grain, heavy pressure gives near-solid
  color. Value comes from pressure, not from adding hues.
- Line: one confident, fine line in a dark cool indigo-brown, never
  black. Natural weight variation (thinner far, thicker near), gentle
  hand wobble under 1 px, tapered ends, tiny overshoot at corners, an
  occasional break. Characters get the heaviest line, background about
  half. Suggest rather than outline everything: fur is a few layered
  scalloped masses with soft textured edges, not every hair.
- Color: fine, even, directional hatching in tight rows with a visible
  tooth sparkle, plus a soft cross-hatch pass on large areas for depth.
  Edges stay clean and controlled.
- Sky and snow: a few large, soft, low-alpha washes (watercolor or
  gouache feel), fading upward, with the pencil grain worked over them.
  Pigment pools very slightly at edges and granulates into the paper.
  Paper white is reserved for the brightest snow and highlights.
- Depth: layered scenery (foreground rocks and snow, characters, ridges,
  sky), slow parallax, atmospheric haze mixing toward one shared cool
  blue. Falling snow in several sizes and depths, soft and slightly
  irregular.
- Occlusion: a nearer shape erases what is behind it inside its
  outline, so no line shows through snow, fur, or the scarf.
- Faces: minimal, dot or simple eyes, no realistic anatomy.

3. COLOR LOGIC
- A limited box of about a dozen pigments, never more.
- Shadows are the local color mixed toward one shared cool violet-blue.
  Distance mixes toward the one haze color.
- A blue-white world. Warm orange appears only on the lantern. At the
  end, the red scarf is the only warm color on screen. Keep the scarf's
  blue muted so the red dominates.
- Color is an event: warmth arrives with a cause (lantern glow, then the
  scarf), and it dies back into blue when the cause ends.

4. CHARACTERS AND THE SCARF
- Tumble, the elf, the scarf, tag, cave, and village match the six
  reference images in proportion, color, and costume shape.
- Tumble: shaggy layered fur masses with a soft textured edge that
  catches snow and ice.
- Scarf: visible stitching, mismatched patches, a clear readable tag,
  so it reads as the same scarf from the first film.
- Small key objects (lantern, scarf, tag) get a faint paper-white halo
  so they read against busy snow.

5. MOTION QUALITY
- All motion is smooth and eased at the full frame rate. Characters are
  never animated at a reduced frame rate.
- Real easing curves and subtle motion blur on camera and characters.
- Handmade life comes only from a very slow, subtle shimmer of pencil
  grain and line edges (under 1 px). Calm it to nearly still during the
  long stillness of Scene 3 and the final hold.
- Invisible forces are drawn: wind as drifting snow streams and curling
  trails, breath as soft fog.
- Colors never snap: light blooms from its source and fades gradually.

6. CAMERA GRAMMAR
Slow, deliberate moves, each with a reason: long holds, slow push-ins,
a patient wide for the distance between the two silhouettes, tight
close-ups for hands, breath, and thread. Subjects fill at least a third
of frame height at story beats. Transitions are match cuts and shared
elements, never dissolves.

7. PITFALLS TO CHECK IN THE STILL
- Pencil that reads as scribble: use tight, even hatching, not loose
  zigzags.
- Light-pressure color going muddy under a wash: raise pressure or
  cross-hatch where the wash crosses.
- Straight clipping edges: clip color by noisy shapes, not rectangles.
- Outlines over fur reading as a headband: outline fur masses on the
  outer edge only.
- Heavy detail competing with the silence and the distance: simplify.
- Clean, glossy gradients: always work pencil grain over the washes.

PART 5: MUSIC DIRECTION
=========================================
PRINCIPLE
The score is the film's narrator. There is no dialogue, so the music tells the audience what Tumble feels, what the elf feels, and how the distance between them is changing. It should be emotionally resonant but restrained: it never tells us to cry, it lets us feel what is already on screen. Silence is part of the score, and where the music stops matters as much as where it plays.

CORE IDEA: ONE INCOMPLETE MELODY
- One simple, lullaby-like melody of about 6 notes (Tumble's theme).
- Across the film it is only ever played incompletely: missing its last note, or stopping on an unresolved note.
- The final note is supplied by the village bells in the last frame, so the melody finally resolves when Tumble is accepted. The bells should echo the bell sound from the first film.
- Unfinished means alone. Completed means belonging.

TONE
- Tender, lonely, quiet, a little awkward, never grand or sweeping
- A gentle winter-lullaby quality that nods to Rankin/Bass, without copying any existing melody
- Minor or modal for loneliness, with one warm major color reserved for the ending
- Slow tempo, about 60 to 70 BPM, with plenty of space between notes

INSTRUMENT PALETTE (all synthesized with Web Audio)
- Music box or celesta: the elf, small and fragile
- Low, soft, filtered pad or cello-like tone: Tumble, large and gentle
- Wind, tuned lightly to the key: the storm, always present underneath
- Bells: reserved for the ending only

RULES

REFERENCE: THE SNOWMAN (1982)
What to borrow:
- One song-like melody carries the whole film
- Withhold the big statement: the full theme is saved for the single moment that earns it. Here, that is the final bell resolution
- Music tracks emotion, not action. Do not score the fall, the tumble, or footsteps as events. Score fear, patience, and trust
- A soft, rolling figure under the simple tune, so the long stillness in Scene 3 feels like it is breathing
- A child-like sound carries the child's perspective. Optional: a very soft wordless voice-like tone for the elf on its own low stem. Cut it if it sounds artificial
- Music withdraws at the saddest moment: silence and wind
What NOT to borrow:
- No soaring, flight-like swell. Ours stays grounded and small
- Do not copy its melody, rhythm, or phrase shape. Analyze how the score is structured (one theme, held back, quiet ending) and write an original theme from those principles, not from its notes
- No comedic or dance-hall cues
- Wonder is not our tone

GOAL FOR THE VIEWER
By the end, the audience should be able to hum the melody and feel that it was waiting for its last note the whole time.

1. Confirm you understand the project in 3 to 4 sentences.
2. Ask me the open questions together: (a) the elf's final clothing (red sock and blue mitten, or blue hat and red socks), (b) the visual style, (c) resolution and frame rate.
3. Then proceed to Step 3: the screenplay and the style bible (palette with hex values, line style, camera rules, sound palette, character construction notes for Tumble, the elf, the scarf, and the ridge).
4. After the style bible, build ONE test shot for me to judge before building any full scene. A good test shot is shot 25 (the approach) or shot 13 (the decision hold), since they carry the film.
	- confirm this style with a single still frame of the ridge scene (shot 13) before building the test shot. That frame has both characters, the distance, and the palette, so it will show quickly whether the look is right.
5. I want the cinematography to include Oscar winning shots that when the audience sees these shots they think “wow these are unique and creative” include 1-2 perfect match cuts that do not feel added just for effect and they contribute to the sentimentality of the story
6. Be honest. If any beat is weak or sentimental, tell me.

Remember, when making this short, please feel free to make it longer if you feel certain shots should hold for longer for emotional effect. DO NOT make the pacing feel rushed. Also especially focus on the musical emotional tone conveyed. This should appear to be a professional level production.
